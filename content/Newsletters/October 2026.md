> [!info]
> ### Top culture shocks
> - **I'm asked "you alright?" in place of "how are you?"** They're functionally the same question but mean the inverse here, the former a casual greeting and the latter a genuine inquiry. It's caught me off-guard a handful of times. The result: an awkward beat in conversation as I wrestle my social reflexes -- I'm fine, do I look not fine? Newt warned me about this one. Had he not, I'd still be fumbling this interaction.
> - **The. Traffic.** I will never again take for granted our wide, Eisenhoweresque roads. Instead, I entrust my (hourlong) commute to the bus drivers who can masterfully squeeze a double decker bus down an alley with 20cm of clearance on each side. 
> - **I was told Brit food sucked.** Frankly, everything has been amazing, and I had a chippie so good that it converted even Mom and Dad. That said, I'm in want of an authentic Mexican restaurant — SOL there.
> - **Does the climate enable smart dress, or is London just an exceptionally fashionable city?** Everyone here is so stylish. I've spent a small fortune converting my casual West coast wardrobe into something that can endure wind, rain, and cold, and look nicely semiprofessional at all times. Gone are the days of athleisure everywhere...
> - **Utterly brutal clock change.** When daylight savings ends, the mood in London tangibly deflates. Correspondingly, we go from the warm and cutesy-sounding British Summer Time (BST) back to grumpy, chilly Greenwich Mean Time (GMT). On the shortest day of the year, [we get a paltry 7 hours and 50 minutes of sun](https://www.maptools.uk/tools/sunrise-sunset/uk-daylight-hours-guide/) :,). 
> - **Christmas is a months-long affair**. As early as my arrival in September I could preorder my Christmas dinner. Living in Phoenix, Christmas came during the seasonable time of year, so it was never a holiday to really cling to; in the depths of winter here, the festivities are absolutely necessary. It's a sentimental, cozy beacon.

Our two days of orientation in Chelsea were peppered with opportunities to meet the other new starters while otherwise being information-dense and unexciting; standard fare for days one and two. Afterwards, some of us went to the Natural History Museum in Kensington, where the skeleton of a blue whale called [Hope](https://www.nhm.ac.uk/discover/hope-the-blue-whale-history.html) hangs from the hall ceiling in front of a statue of Charles Darwin. The [significance of these symbols](https://www.the-scientist.com/peto-s-paradox-how-gigantic-species-evolved-to-beat-cancer-72725) was overt enough to nearly be funny.

![[Pasted image 20261102233201.png]]

I've gotten to know many fellow students living in the [Tooting](https://www.visitlondon.com/things-to-do/london-areas/tooting) (yes, seriously) student accommodation well. Many of us commute and take lunch together; we've been climbing and playing discount [padel](https://www.youtube.com/shorts/bpUa5FB0lIw). As the token American[^1], it took little time for me to take on Thanksgiving planning. It will be a feat if I can manage a turkey in my little Euro oven. Check back next month.

Outings this month have included the awesome [Spitalfields](https://www.spitalfields.co.uk/market/), [Borough](https://boroughmarket.org.uk/), and [Brick Lane Vintage](https://www.vintage-market.co.uk/) Markets; the first was probably my favorite, being a good mix of actual artisan goods and food booths — very few cheap reseller stands. Expecting a visit in June from my cousin who is hellbent on thrifting, I've done my due diligence to scout out spots for us to visit. Friends brought me to [Camden Town](https://www.visitlondon.com/things-to-do/london-areas/camden-town), which is officially as far North as I've managed to wander. Its tour boats navigating canal water locks and willow trees were very charming. We had a Halloween party at the bar on campus that resembles something of a Moose Lodge, and afterward I went to a friend's flat for games and drinks. Despite Halloween being a lesser affair here compared to home, I had a good holiday dressed as a devil.

![[Pasted image 20261102222419.png]]

As for the lab, I am fortunate for a few reasons: 
- our building is brand new, 
- we have an absolutely enormous team (twenty-five and counting!),
- and, in late October, Trevor was awarded an *absolutely enormous* grant: the €14 million multi-PI [ERC Synergy grant](https://erc.europa.eu/apply-grant/synergy-grant).

Last week, I attended a conference on genome instability that was hosted at [The Royal Society.](https://royalsociety.org/) As in, *The* Royal Society — the one to which Newton, Darwin, Hooke, Einstein, Turing and others[^3] belong. The oldest scientific academy in continuous existence, founded 366 years ago this month. Getting there, I passed through Trafalgar Square, and on my lunch I walked to Bucking ham Palace. There were tourists on my commute. I can't explain how surreal this still is to me — not to mention that the research presented was absolutely dizzying as well. I saw everything from exciting clinical trial results to the minutia of structural biology.
![[Pasted image 20261102230627.png]]

I'm surrounded by people much smarter than myself. It's been a few weeks of realizing that I don't know nearly as much as I thought I did, and that everything I thought I was capable of has already been done. Trevor warned me of the first-month freakout, which I am currently undergoing. Alas, a familiar course to anyone in higher ed. It means that making something out of this will be that much more satisfying :).

I've also been thinking on an essay from a man called Christopher Ingraham who was diagnosed with and treated for terminal bile duct cancer; through a series of serendipitous medical events, his doctors at Mayo would discover he'd actually had a very treatable, albeit infinitesimally rare, lymphoma. Ingraham writes a measured piece that illustrates how medical decisions are careful assessments of risk and benefit moreso than the definitive action we'd like them to be.
*What assumptions can you make to treat someone if a direct biopsy risks disease progression?* 
It's a heartwrenching yet beautiful article that stuck with me. I'm quite keen on patient stories to remind me what's actually on the line in my day-to-day work. [Here's the unpaywalled Slate link.](https://archive.is/ESgMV)
As far as movies and tv go, I'm waiting for You Can See Everything to premiere here. Hank Green did a [good interview with the Theranos whistleblower](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=d9EaqyoA29E) Tyler Shultz that serves a clear picture into the innards of biotech. Bit technical, but worth a watch.

---
Running is a quintessential grad school activity (next to climbing, board games, and cycling) which I've rekindled an interest in. I say interest and not fondness because I hate running. Worse yet, I feel so much better after my run. I can't even make an excuse not to. However, I'm aching for the massive industrial gyms back home; I can't find a large gym with a sauna[^2] for the life of me. I'd never thought I'd say it, but I miss the Guadalupe EOS. Why does America's hottest city have better sauna infrastructure than London?

So, what do I miss? I don't miss having a car or driving, but I do miss the agency of being able to go on road trips. I would do nearly anything for a day in Payson — any wilderness — right now. I miss clear skies and temps in the high 60s. I miss October in Arizona; it's a cruelty that I had to miss my favorite month of the year back home. I miss Denver and its mountains. I miss swimming, and I miss all of you!

All in all, I feel grateful every day for friends, this job, this city, and this life. I heard someone say that living in London really challenges you, and I think it will.
