(*Most of this information upfront comes from [a curated thread by u/SplurgyA on r/HobbyDrama](https://www.reddit.com/r/HobbyDrama/comments/mxcq2i/video_game_creatures_or_how_the_us_navy/)*)

I recently watched a [video essay on Creatures](https://youtu.be/IDxFxWakhm0?si=ubZ8xBTN06UbKjHQ), a 90's simulation game that tasked its players with the care and keeping of little creatures called Norns. What made Creatures (game) so special was that its creatures (Norns) were simple neural networks capable of learning and producing emergent behavior — see a testimonial from someone whose Norns learned to play catch on their own, despite never been explicitly programmed to do so. The creator of Creatures, Steve Grand, endowed them with some wicked synthetic biology: not only were they capable of "cognition," they had reward and hormone systems, rudimentary biochemical systems and extensive genomes with capacity for breeding, leading players to engineer extensive pedigrees. Norns need your guidance and encouragement. Norns can get sick. Norns can learn language. Norns can die a lonely death! Naturally, players (which in the fandom are referred to as Breeders, but I hate that, and will not be using it) become very attached to their Norns. What's more, you can export and import your Norns to share with others. Upload and duplicate their consciousness [a la MMAcevedo](https://qntm.org/mmacevedo). Neat.

Most players choose to play a benevolent God for their Norns. Obviously, however, a small subset choose to fill their Norns' artificial existences with misery and pain. One [AntiNorn](https://themagnet.substack.com/p/the-magnet-25-is-it-ok-to-torture) became widely acknowledged for his prolific torture of his Norns, and ran a website dedicated to sharing files of Norns he'd systematically abused, meeting fellow Anti-Norns and challenging others to rehabilitate his broken Norns. One of his — victims? — was a Norn named [Slave](http://web.archive.org/web/20021230200936im_/http://www.geocities.com/SiliconValley/Park/2495/slave.jpg), who, among other afflictions, would stumble around drunkenly as AntiNorn had engineered her genome to give her [autobrewery syndrome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Auto-brewery_syndrome). Any players who downloaded Slave and attempted to give her a decent life by allowing her to procreate would find their careful pedigrees poisoned by her boozy genes. A Trojan Norn.

Of course, the Creatures Community descended on the guy. I won't postulate about his motivations for his digital sadism — the guy was a US Navyman, make of it what you will — but to some, his torture of artificial lifeforms was a transgression worthy of death. His inbox contained fire and hate, [but also threats to inject his eyeballs with hydrogen peroxide, slash his face and carve out his brain, and acid-etch his bowels](https://web.archive.org/web/20031202220456/http://www.geocities.com/siliconvalley/park/2495/email.html), among other colorful imaginings. Some aren't willing to forget AntiNorm's crimes, even now as the story's resurfaced in various corners of the internet. But the extent to which people feel that the two equate morally — maltreatment of digital pets and genuine death threats — *actually* shocks me. 

I'm reminded of an anecdote from (I think?) an anthropology professor: Holds up a pencil, waving it around and saying "Hello! I'm Timothy! I love to help kids write and draw!" before snapping it in half to the shock and dismay of the class*. We *love* to relate ourselves to things. More, we love to attribute humanness to nonhuman entities. That's why grandma makes the dog talk, and people date AIs, and why people are insane online about their [blorbos](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/blorbo_from_my_shows) — meaning favorite online personas, celebrities, and fictional characters. The term [parasocial relationship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parasocial_interaction) gets thrown around a lot, and I think it captures the illusion of the connection, but not the extent to which people invest themselves in, ally with in their blorbos. Thinking Markiplier wants you is one thing; tying your wellbeing to Markiplier and avowing to defend his persona at every impasse online is another.

All this gets to: I grew up lurking (and continue to lurk) in fandom with this unease to engage myself for fear of ending on the wrong side of shipping wars or other intrafandom drama that conflate your moral character or political lean with your faves. I'm *not* saying it's not that deep. [It is](https://youtu.be/riU59TBB8W0?si=hEgeYUXjUXBntQxv), in the sense that fiction is a mirror. But I think it's easy to deeply misunderstand to point of fiction, or of spectacle, when it feels that real. Why do we watch thrillers? F1? Murder mysteries? A steamy affair between gorgeous hockey players? A guy free solo Taipei Tower? It's a sandbox for experiences where no one gets hurt. 

Finally, the recent discourse on Pitt Twitter. Langdon defenders are going down in blazing gunfire ("TEAM LANGDON LOST AL-HASHIMI"). Team Santos is fielded by the insufferable bully-turned-nurse type. Blatant character misreads assail both sides like missiles ("She stole the scalpel to frame Langdon for something") ("I know who she voted for"). We refer to the characters by their first names, our close personal friends Dennis and Trinity and Frank and Baran. Dare dislike my blorbo? Hydrogen peroxide for thee.
